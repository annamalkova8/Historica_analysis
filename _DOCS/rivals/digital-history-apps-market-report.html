Summary

  • Our edge is the method. Historica builds borders with AI and algorithms. No rival advertises this. The downside: our own site says the maps may be inaccurate.
  • Map plus time slider is common. Geacron, Chronas, OpenHistoricalMap and paid iOS atlases all offer it. We must stand out on trust and tools.
  • Mobile is open ground. We have no native app. Paid iOS atlases stop at 1900. Geacron's Android app was last updated in 2018.
  • Layers help us. Users can switch countries, regions, cities, tribes and events on and off, and find a specific object in the search panel. Most atlases show borders only.
  • Newer small sites win on habit, not depth. Panchronia and Natagant offer daily games, quizzes and printable posters. We have none of that.
  • Map Builder is our best lever, but it is invite-only while rivals already let anyone edit.

Wikipedia links are common (Geacron, Chronas, TimeMap.org, Histography), so they are expected rather than a differentiator.

A built-in screenshot is useful for teachers and students making slides, and for sharing on social media. I could not confirm it in rivals, though Geacron offers image and data export (KML, shapefile).

We lead on: the longest continuous range with borders to the present (4000 BCE to today), monthly resolution, coverage to the present, five switchable layers (countries, regions, cities, tribes, events), a search panel for finding specific objects, Wikipedia links in events, a built-in screenshot option, open method notes, and a scalable data engine.
We trail on: mobile, open editing, embeds and export, and a paid tier. We also have no real historical documents; archives like OldMapsOnline and Rumsey own that.

What this means for us:

  • They do not match our depth or monthly time steps. Panchronia stops at 2012.
  • They are ahead on engagement: games, quizzes, shareable "paradoxes" and printables give people a reason to return.
  • Panchronia is clear about where its borders come from and under what licence. That is the trust message we lack.
  • Both target Russian speakers directly. An older developer article complained that localised history tools were scarce, so this niche is being filled. Our language support is not verified.

Other similar products: TimeMap.org (regions, rulers, people and battles with Wikipedia links) and Histography (a self-updating Wikipedia timeline with 18 categories). Neither is a threat on borders, but both show how simple, free tools gain attention.

Map Builder (closed beta)

Map Builder lets users edit borders, names and cities (the same layers as on the main map), add their own sources, and build custom timelines. Access is limited to researchers with a Master's or PhD. Our tech notes suggest that adding a source point can redraw borders automatically. Chronas and OHM make users edit shapes by hand, so this is a real difference (my reading of the notes; please verify).

Strengths: unique "edit the evidence, borders follow" idea; starts from a full map; a natural paid feature.

Risks:

  • The announcement invites teachers and enthusiasts, but the beta gate excludes them.
  • Rivals already collect contributions from open communities.
  • User scenarios next to scholarly maps could blur trust unless labelled.
  • The team lists stability as ongoing work, so opening too fast could hurt.

Suggested opening path:

1. Deepen beta20 to 50 researchers. Case studies. Fix stability and data upload.
2. WaitlistPublic form. Invite teachers in batches. Add share links. Label "Historica model" vs "User version".
3. Open with limitsFree tier plus Pro (export, embeds). Institutional licences.

Marketing line to test: "Don't just view history. Rebuild it with your sources."

Limits

The layer list, search panel, Wikipedia links and screenshot option come from the product team, not from my own testing of the map.
